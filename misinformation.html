Linguistic markers of misinformation

Do lexical patterns, syntactic features, and sentiment help distinguish misinformation from credible political content?

Overview

Course
DSAN 5000, Fall 2024
With
Jonah Lichtenthal
Data
The LIAR dataset of fact-checked short statements; exploratory collection via News API, Media Cloud, and the All the News dataset
Materials

RepositoryProject reportProject website

Methods

  • Classification — TF-IDF lexical features and a Random Forest classifier for LIAR’s six truthfulness categories.
  • Linguistic analysis — part-of-speech counts and lexical feature importance.
  • Sentiment — VADER and a DistilBERT-based sentiment pipeline, examined across truthfulness categories.
  • Exploratory structure — topic modeling and dimensionality reduction, including NMF and t-SNE.

DistilBERT was used for sentiment analysis, not as a directly compared misinformation classifier.

Results and limitations

These are the results recorded in the project report for the six-class Random Forest evaluation — the originally reported results, not a newly reproduced benchmark.

The modest performance makes the limits of surface linguistic cues central to the project. Patterns associated with dataset labels do not establish whether an unfamiliar claim is true, and results on short fact-checked statements do not demonstrate generalization to full news articles.

My contribution

My work included data collection and configurable news-gathering functions, supervised learning, sentiment analysis, and exploratory analysis. I also contributed the report’s supervised learning, results and analysis, and conclusion sections, and organized the project website.

What it led to

The project sharpened a question I now pursue directly: what a measurable textual pattern shows about meaning, and what further evidence an interpretation needs.
