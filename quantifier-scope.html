Quantifier scope across languages

Can a preference for interpreting a sentence in one language carry over to another? And what would it mean for a language model to show a similar pattern?

The starting observation

My notes begin with pairs of sentences involving universal and existential expressions. An English example makes the distinction concrete:

Every student read a book.

Possibly different books
For every student, there is a book that they read. The sentence does not require all students to have read the same book.
One shared book
There is a particular book that every student read.

The point of interest is not simply that two readings can be described. It is which reading people prefer, under what conditions, and how we would establish that preference.

A cross-linguistic question

My original note places Chinese examples alongside English ones, including “每个学生读了一本书” and “一个学生读了每本书”. That comparison raises a question: how should we distinguish the contribution of sentence structure from the effects of context and a speaker’s language background?

These examples are a starting point, not evidence that corresponding sentences have identical interpretation patterns. A literature review and careful assessment of the materials would need to precede any experiment.

A possible investigation

One proposed approach is to compare interpretation choices across controlled contexts. The design would need to separate several factors:

  • Sentence and context: vary supporting context while keeping the relevant lexical content as consistent as possible.
  • Language background: document proficiency and language experience rather than treating bilingual speakers as a uniform group.
  • Task: compare interpretations using a clearly specified judgment or scenario-selection task.
  • Model comparison: document prompts and model settings, and check whether the response format itself changes the pattern.

What would count as an answer?

An observed preference would require an explanation that considers the task and the materials. Similar choices by people and models would not, by themselves, demonstrate a shared processing mechanism. Differences might also reflect the evaluation setup rather than a general difference in linguistic competence.

For now, the aim is to turn an intuition into a tractable question: what evidence would let us distinguish the competing explanations?

Status: an early research sketch, developed from a notebook question. It led to ScopeShift, which narrows the question to one English construction and to language models; the cross-linguistic and human comparisons described here have not been run.
