Talking online

Gems among the fragments

4 November 2021

Contents

  2. Toward others
  3. If you take it seriously, you lose
  4. Relentless warmth
  5. Where is the friend?

Online conversation goes wrong in such regular ways that the failures start to look less like accidents and more like rules. I have been collecting them. Here is the list so far, with a title borrowed from the habit of finding something worth keeping in the debris: gems among the fragments.

Toward oneself

The first set of mistakes is about how people see their own behaviour.

  • Mistaking forcefulness for power. The loudest reply in a thread often looks as if it is winning. But volume is not authority. Nobody has conceded anything; they have just stopped answering.
  • Mistaking anonymity for an advantage. Without a name there are no consequences, and that feels like freedom. It also means that nothing you say is backed by anything. An anonymous voice is the easiest kind to ignore.
  • Mistaking hostility for humour. A cruel remark with “just kidding” attached is still a cruel remark. The joke is a shield for the speaker, not a gift to the listener.
  • Mistaking flippancy for friendliness. Being breezy with a stranger is meant to seem approachable. It often reads as not taking them seriously.

Toward others

The second set is about how people read everyone else.

  • Mistaking showing for showing off. Someone posts a photo of something they made. Readers see a performance.
  • Mistaking sharing for bragging. Someone shares good news. Readers hear a boast.
  • Mistaking modesty for mockery. “I just got lucky” is read as sarcasm aimed at those who didn’t.
  • Mistaking a question for a rhetorical one. “Why did you do it that way?” is asked out of curiosity and heard as “You shouldn’t have.”
  • Mistaking a rhetorical question for a taunt. And when a question really is rhetorical, it is heard as an attack.

Put the lists side by side and a pattern appears. Every misreading moves in the same direction: away from the generous interpretation and toward the suspicious one. Modesty is never mistaken for sincerity; it is mistaken for mockery. A question is never mistaken for something kinder than it is.

It is worth saying that the two lists are not about two different kinds of people. The same person who mistakes their own hostility for humour in one thread will mistake someone else’s modesty for mockery in the next. Most of us are, on different days, on both sides of the list.

Part of the reason is the medium. Face to face, a question comes with a tone of voice, a facial expression, a history between the people talking. In a comment box, all of that is gone, and the reader has to fill the gap. Online, the gap is usually filled with suspicion.

If you take it seriously, you lose

The internet has its own conclusion to draw from all this, and it has become a kind of motto: if you take it seriously, you lose.

It is a defensive stance. If you are never earnest, you can never be caught out. Whatever you said, you were only half-joking. Whatever was said to you, you never really cared. In a place where every sincere remark risks being read as mockery, refusing sincerity looks like the safe choice.

But the motto has a cost. If everyone follows it, sincere conversation becomes impossible, because nobody can tell who is being serious. The rule that protects each person individually makes the whole space worse for everyone. A forum where nobody is ever earnest is a forum where nothing can be learned, because learning starts with someone admitting, in earnest, that they don’t know.

Relentless warmth

There is one more feature that the lists don’t capture. Almost everyone online tries very hard to seem warm. Messages arrive buried in stickers and emoji. Sentences end in strings of cute particles and typed-out giggles. Tildes trail off the end of every line, as if each sentence were being sung~~~

I think this is the other side of the misreadings. If plain text is read as cold, or as hostile, then the natural response is to pad it with signals of friendliness. The trouble is that warmth used as decoration stops meaning anything. When every message is covered in hearts, the hearts no longer tell you how the writer feels. They only tell you that the writer is afraid of being misread.

So the effort to seem warm and the habit of reading coldly feed each other. The more people expect hostility, the more they decorate; the more they decorate, the less any decoration counts.

Where is the friend?

Chinese has a word for the people you meet online: wangyou, literally “net friends”. After making this list, I find the word hard to say with a straight face. They call them net friends, but they are mostly rubbish. Where on the net is there a friend?
