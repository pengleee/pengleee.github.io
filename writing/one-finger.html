One finger

On the Book of Changes, and the trouble with rich interpretability and nonlinear thinking

21 November 2021

Contents

  2. A prophecy that cannot fail
  3. An old trick
  4. The trouble with rich interpretability
  5. The middle finger

There is an old joke I keep returning to whenever I open the Book of Changes. It is short, it is crude, and it says more about that ancient book than many serious commentaries do.

The joke

Three young men are on their way to the capital to sit the imperial examination, the test that decided, for centuries, who would enter government service in China. On the road they pass a Taoist temple. They buy incense, go inside, and ask the master the question every examinee wants answered: how many of us will pass?

The master says nothing. He raises one finger. They ask what he means, and he tells them that the secrets of heaven cannot be revealed. Satisfied, or at least impressed, they thank him and continue on their way.

When they have gone, a young disciple comes over, puzzled.

“With one finger, you can only mean that one of them will pass. What if two of them pass?”

“Then one of them fails.”

“And if none of the three passes?”

“Then not one of them passes.”

“And if all three pass?”

“Fool. Then not one of them fails.”

The disciple is delighted. “So that is the secret of heaven!”

“Secret of heaven, my foot,” says the master. “I was giving them the middle finger. Good scholars, and they come running to have their fortunes told. They are being swindled and don’t even know it.”

A prophecy that cannot fail

Before the punchline, the joke has already made its point. There are only four things that can happen to three candidates: one passes, two pass, none pass, or all pass. For every one of them, the master has a sentence ready that contains the word “one”. Whatever the results say when they are posted, the finger will turn out to have been right.

That is the whole trick. The finger looks like a prediction, but it commits to nothing. The three young men leave believing they have been told something about their future. What they have actually been given is a blank form, which they will fill in themselves once the results are out. Whoever passes will remember the master fondly. Whoever fails will find that the finger meant him too.

A real prediction is one that could turn out wrong. “Two of you will pass” sticks its neck out: if all three pass, or only one, the prophet is exposed. The finger never sticks its neck out, and that is exactly why it can never be caught.

An old trick

The master is in distinguished company. The most famous version of the same move comes from ancient Greece. According to Herodotus, King Croesus of Lydia asked the oracle at Delphi whether he should go to war with Persia, and was told that if he crossed the river Halys, he would destroy a great empire. He crossed it, and he did destroy a great empire: his own.

Modern horoscopes work along the same lines, in a gentler way. They are written so that almost anyone can see themselves in them: you can be sociable but sometimes need time alone, you are hard on yourself, you have potential you haven’t used yet. Psychologists have a name for our readiness to accept such descriptions as personal: the Barnum effect, after the showman who promised something for everyone.

The structure is always the same. The speaker keeps the words loose, the listener tightens them after the fact, and then the listener gives the credit to the speaker.

The trouble with rich interpretability

The Book of Changes is, in a sense, the grandest version of this structure ever written. Its line statements are short and full of images: waiting on the sand, a dragon in the field, a fox wetting its tail as it crosses a river. A single line can be read as encouragement or as a warning, depending on what the reader brings to it. Commentators have added layer upon layer of interpretation for more than two thousand years, and the text has room for all of it.

That openness is what makes the book endlessly re-readable. It is also exactly what a swindler needs. A text that can support any reading can be made to confirm any outcome.

The deeper problem is the direction of the reasoning. In ordinary thinking we move forward, from evidence to conclusion: I studied, so I will probably pass. With a text that can mean anything, the direction reverses. The outcome comes first, and the reading is chosen afterwards to fit it. Once you start reading that way, nothing can ever count against the text, and so nothing in the text can ever teach you anything you had not already decided.

This is the sense in which rich interpretability becomes a problem rather than a richness. A book that can always be right can no longer tell you when you are wrong.

The middle finger

The joke’s final turn is the best part. Even the gesture was ambiguous. The three candidates saw a number. The disciple saw a riddle with four answers. The master meant an insult.

And the insult was aimed not at fortune-telling itself, but at the people who should have known better. These were scholars, men who had spent years with classical texts, on their way to an examination that tested their judgment. They knew how to read. Yet at the moment of greatest uncertainty, they handed their reasoning over to a raised finger and paid for the privilege.

The only person in the story who learns anything is the disciple, and he learns it by doing what the scholars did not: asking what the answer would have been if things had gone differently. The master’s reply to that question is the real secret of heaven in this joke. There wasn’t one.
