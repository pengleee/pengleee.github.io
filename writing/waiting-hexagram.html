Thoughts on Xu (䷄), the hexagram of waiting

18 November 2021

Contents
  1. What a hexagram is
  2. A river crossing in six pictures
  3. Time as timing
  4. Many readings, and how to choose

Today I read the first five hexagrams of the Book of Changes: Qian, Kun, Zhun, Meng and Xu. I skimmed most of them, but I read Xu closely, and it moved me more than the others.

What a hexagram is

For anyone who hasn’t opened the book, a quick orientation. The Book of Changes, or Yijing, is built from sixty-four figures called hexagrams. Each hexagram is a stack of six lines, and each line is either solid or broken. A hexagram can also be read as two smaller figures of three lines each, called trigrams, one sitting on top of the other. Each hexagram has a name, a short judgment, and a statement for each of its six lines, read from the bottom up. Around this ancient core, later writers added commentaries that explain and expand on it.

Xu (䷄) is water over heaven: the trigram for water sits above the trigram for heaven. One of the commentaries reads the figure as clouds rising into the sky. Rain is coming, but it has not come yet. So the hexagram is about waiting, and its name means exactly that.

䷄Xu · Waiting

With sincerity, bright success. Persistence brings good fortune. It is favourable to cross the great river.

first line
Waiting in the outskirts. It is favourable to persevere; no blame.
second line
Waiting on the sand. There is some small talk; in the end, good fortune.
third line
Waiting in the mud brings the bandits.
fourth line
Waiting in blood. Get out of the pit.
fifth line
Waiting over wine and food. Persistence brings good fortune.
top line
Entering the pit. Three uninvited guests arrive; honour them, and in the end, good fortune.

A river crossing in six pictures

What struck me most is how vivid the line statements are. Read from bottom to top, they are almost a picture-log of fording a great river, which is exactly what the judgment says is favourable.

First you wait in the outskirts, far from the water, where nothing can go wrong as long as you stay steady. Then you wait on the sand of the riverbank. There is some talk, some friction, but it passes. Then you are in the mud at the water’s edge, stuck, and the commentary is blunt: by stopping there you invite the bandits yourself. Then you are in blood, in real danger, and the advice is simply to get out of the pit. Then comes waiting over wine and food: patience without anxiety, at ease with the delay. And finally you go down into the pit after all, uninvited guests arrive, and if you treat them with respect it ends well.

In our general education course on the Changes, the teacher told us that every hexagram can be understood through “image thinking”. The trouble is that people today live so far from the images of the natural world, from riverbanks and mud and rain clouds, that it is hard to grasp them from within. Reading Xu, I think I understood what our teacher meant. The text does not explain waiting; it shows you where you are standing while you wait.

Time as timing

The biggest thing I took from Xu is waiting: biding your time until the moment is right. The Changes puts great weight on timing. Time here is not the objective, evenly flowing quantity of Newton’s absolute space and time, the same everywhere and for everyone. Time means the moment: what to do at what time, in a way that fits the person’s position.

The ancient Greeks had two words for time that capture the difference. Chronos is clock time, one second after another. Kairos is the opportune moment, the time that is right for something. The Changes is almost entirely a book about kairos.

Seen this way, waiting is not the same as doing nothing. The six lines show waiting done well and waiting done badly. Waiting steadily in the outskirts is blameless; waiting stuck in the mud invites disaster; waiting over wine and food, calmly, is what brings good fortune. The question is never only whether to wait, but where you are standing while you do it.

Many readings, and how to choose

The line statements are vague, and that vagueness leaves enormous room for interpretation. The Ten Wings, the classical commentaries that tradition attributes to Confucius, are themselves only a few of the possible interpretations. Every age since has added its own.

But here is the mysterious part. Faced with the Changes, people do not simply take whatever they want. Readers find something in the statements that resonates with their own situation, and beyond that, they come away with real insight. The text is open, but it is not empty.

So how do you catch the right reading among ten thousand possible ones? What matters, in the end, is cultivating virtue. Casting lots and shaking out hexagrams is the lesser part, the tool. Moral cultivation is the higher part, the Way. Xunzi put it in a single line: those who are good at the Changes do not divine. Only when one’s virtue reaches a certain level can one hope for the unity of heaven and humanity, and hope to know the mandate of heaven.
