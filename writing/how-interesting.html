Essay

“How interesting”

24 October 2023

Contents

  2. What tone used to do
  3. Memes as shorthand
  4. From ambiguity to antagonism

Chinese is a poetic language. It can say a great deal in very few words, and much of its beauty lies in what it leaves for the reader to fill in. But that same vagueness has a cost. It can sharpen the friction between people, because the same sentence can be understood in many ways in different contexts, sometimes in opposite ways. And online, where conversation is carried mostly by short fragments of text, the problem gets worse.

One comment, three meanings

Here is a small example. Suppose I read an article and leave a two-word comment underneath it: zhen you yisi, roughly “how interesting”.

(1)真有意思。 ‘How interesting.’

praise
The article is valuable and worth reading.
novelty
The article takes a strikingly original angle.
contempt
The article is garbage, and the fact that anyone wrote it at all is “really something”.

Intuitively, it is very hard to tell which of these I meant. The words are identical in all three cases. The first is a compliment, the second is curiosity, and the third is an insult wearing the clothes of a compliment.

What tone used to do

If I said the same two words to you in person, you would almost never be confused. You would hear my tone: warm, surprised, or dripping with irony. You would see my face. You would know something about me and about what I usually think of articles like this one. Most of the time, spoken ambiguity is resolved before anyone notices it was there.

Fragmentary text strips all of that away. A comment under an article arrives without a voice, without a face and without a shared history. The writer is a stranger and the reader is a stranger. The only things left to decide the meaning are the words, and the words, as we have seen, cannot decide it.

Spoken Chinese, in particular, leans heavily on what text cannot carry. A great deal of attitude lives in the little particles at the end of a sentence and in the rise and fall of the voice: the same words with a different final sound can be warm, impatient, teasing or cold. Written down without them, a sentence becomes flat, and a flat sentence can be read in any direction.

People online know this, and they try to put the missing tone back with emoji and stickers. But the repair does not hold. On the Chinese internet, the little smiling face, the most harmless-looking emoji of all, came to be read as a sign of contempt: a polite smile with nothing behind it, or worse. The very tool meant to settle the meaning became one more thing to read two ways.

There is also the matter of audience. When I say something in person, I say it to particular people. A comment under an article is read by everyone who passes: friends and strangers, people who agree with the article and people who despise it. I cannot choose who reads my two words, and each of them will read the words for themselves.

So the reader decides instead, and the reader decides on the basis of what they already think. If the comment seems to come from someone on their side, it reads as praise. If it seems to come from the other side, it reads as contempt. The ambiguity doesn’t disappear; it becomes a mirror for whatever the reader expected to find.

Memes as shorthand

Memes and running jokes make this worse, especially when they are overused. A meme starts as a shared joke inside a community, a compressed reference that everyone in the group understands. Its whole point is to say a lot in very little. But when a meme spreads beyond the group that made it, it keeps its compression and loses its context. The same phrase can mean affectionate teasing to one group and a calculated insult to another.

Overuse wears memes down further. Once a phrase has been repeated thousands of times, its literal meaning fades, and what is left is mostly a signal of which side the speaker is on. At that point, using it is less like saying something and more like waving a flag.

From ambiguity to antagonism

Put these pieces together, and you get something larger than a misunderstanding between two people. Vague phrases that each group reads in its own way, and memes that work more as badges than as statements, give every group online a steady supply of evidence that the other side is hostile. Each misreading confirms the last one. And because nothing was ever said plainly, nothing can be plainly taken back. The writer who meant praise has no way to prove it; the reader who saw contempt has no reason to doubt it.

This vagueness and ambiguity, together with the overuse of certain memes, deepens the antagonism between groups online. A language’s poetry, in the wrong medium, becomes fuel.
