Language stages abroad

29 November 2023

Contents

  2. Accepting
  3. Switching freely
  4. The shape of the list

Living abroad, I once tried to sum up how my relationship with my languages had changed. It came out as a list of three lines:

  1. Reject my mother tongue and shed everything Chinese.
  2. Accept Chinese.
  3. Switch freely between Chinese and English.

These three stages are a record of my own experience, not a rule about how bilingualism works. I wrote them down because they are what living abroad has taught me so far. And none of the three lines mentions how good my English was. The list is not about proficiency at all. It is about relationship.

Rejecting

The first line is the most absolute. It does not say “speak less Chinese” or “practise more English”. It says to reject the mother tongue, and beyond that, to shed everything Chinese, as if it were something attached to the self from outside.

There was a reason for it. Chinese students abroad form a very large community. Wherever you go, they make a kind of mobile China, and what holds it together above all is speaking Chinese. It is easy to arrive in a new country and never really leave the old one.

When I first went to the UK, it was my first long stay abroad on my own, and I had little confidence in my English, especially my spoken English. I wanted to integrate quickly, and I chose the most drastic method I could think of: tear the old down to build the new. The logic is a familiar one. People learning a language are often told that immersion is everything, and that the fastest way to learn is to cut off the language you already have.

For me it worked rather well. I was on an exchange programme, so my classmates came from all over the world, and I kept to my strategy of shedding Chinese on purpose. By the end I knew very few Chinese students. I had become good friends with classmates from Australia, France and Korea, and got on well with many of my roommates, who were British. In a small way, I had become part of life in Britain.

But the strategy has a premise that is worth looking at. It treats two languages as rivals: every hour spent in one is an hour taken from the other, and every Chinese word is a sign that you have not yet fully arrived. That premise held as long as my surroundings allowed it.

Accepting

The second line is much shorter: accept Chinese. There is no more talk of “everything”.

This stage began when I moved to Irvine, in California. Irvine has a large Asian population, much of it Chinese, and even my roommates were Chinese students. Shedding Chinese was no longer something I could do even if I wanted to.

What changed my mind, though, was not only that the strategy had become impossible. I noticed that many Chinese people who had lived in America for years still used a great deal of Chinese. That was simply how things were, and there was no point resisting it. Speaking Chinese is an advantage, after all, and all the more so when it is your mother tongue. I also noticed that many of them could switch freely between Chinese and English, and it struck me that this kind of bilingualism, not the absence of Chinese, was what a really high level looked like.

And my English was still far from a native speaker’s. So why put on airs? Better to accept Chinese openly, and to give it back its place as my mother tongue.

Acceptance is not the same as going back. The second stage does not undo the first, as if the time spent in English had been a mistake. It simply stops the fight. Chinese is no longer an obstacle to overcome but a fact to live with, and then something to value again.

Switching freely

The third line describes what linguists call code-switching: moving between two languages within a single conversation, sometimes within a single sentence. For a long time code-switching was seen as a sign of weakness, as if a bilingual reached for the other language only when they ran out of words in this one. Linguists now largely see it the other way round. Switching well is a skill. It follows rules, and it uses both languages as resources: one word may be more precise, another more intimate, another simply funnier.

Unlike the first two lines, the third is not something I have reached. It is the stage I am still working towards. My English is better than it was before I went abroad, but I am still a long way from switching freely. My inner speech, the voice I think in, is still Mandarin, and Mandarin is still my dominant language.

I have also noticed that a language needs to be warmed up. After a long stretch of speaking only English, English becomes natural and fluent, even a little internalized. But that produces a kind of linguistic inertia. When I then have to change languages, I am clumsy for a while, even when the language I am switching back to is Chinese, my mother tongue.

Psycholinguists have a name for part of this. They measure what they call switch costs: bilinguals tend to be slower and make more slips right after changing languages than when they stay in one. A language that has been in use is easier to reach for; one that has been set aside takes effort to bring back. What I had experienced as clumsiness is, it turns out, ordinary for anyone who lives in two languages. The people who switch freely have not escaped the cost. They have practised paying it until it is very small.

That is why I think free switching takes study and training. It does not arrive on its own, just from living abroad long enough. The important word in the third line is freely. In the first stage, choosing English was an obligation, a matter of principle. In the third, the choice would be just a choice: neither language the enemy, neither the default, and moving between them no longer something that has to be fought for.

The shape of the list

Read together, the three lines describe a familiar movement: a strong position, a reaction against it, and then something that holds both. Rejection says there can be only one language. Acceptance says there are two after all. Free switching says the two do not have to compete.

The first two lines record what has happened. The third records where I am trying to go: from treating my languages as a contest to treating them as a range.
