Language is a spell, language is a wall

11 February 2023

Contents

  2. A barrier of culture, psychology and values
  3. A thin wall and a thick one
  4. What culture shock is

Korean. Chinese. English and French, and the other languages written in the Latin alphabet. Living abroad, I have been thinking about these three groups, and about what actually stands between them.

Not a barrier to communication

People usually talk about a “language barrier” as if it were a wall that stops you from communicating. I have come to think that is the least important thing a language barrier does.

You can communicate a surprising amount without a shared language. Pointing, gestures, facial expressions, drawing on a napkin: with body language alone, a great deal gets across. A hungry traveller can order a meal anywhere in the world. And now there is powerful translation software as well. Point a phone at a menu or a sign and the words rearrange themselves into your own language. Speak into it and it speaks back. As a barrier to getting a message from one person to another, language is weaker than it has ever been.

And yet the barrier is still there. You can understand every word of a conversation and still not understand what happened in it.

A barrier of culture, psychology and values

What language really walls off is culture, psychology and values. Every language comes with assumptions built into it: about who you are allowed to address in what way, about what has to be said and what is better left unsaid, about how directly you can disagree, about how much of yourself you show to someone you have just met.

Think of how people address each other. In Korean and Japanese, the grammar of a sentence changes depending on who you are talking to: speech levels and honorific forms mark whether the listener is older or younger, senior or junior, close or distant. You cannot even say “Did you eat?” without first deciding where you stand. Chinese marks these relations less in grammar, but it marks them in vocabulary: a classmate a year above you is your “elder brother” or “elder sister” of study; your teacher is never addressed by their given name. In English, a student may call a professor by their first name, and a junior employee may do the same with the head of the company. Translation software will render all of these words correctly. It cannot transplant the whole web of relationships that the words come from.

Or think of how people say no. Across much of East Asia, a direct refusal can feel harsh, and people often decline by not quite declining: they will think about it, it might be difficult, perhaps another time. A listener who shares the culture hears the no immediately. A listener who doesn’t hears a maybe, and waits for an answer that is never coming. In the other direction, a perfectly ordinary English “No, I don’t think that’s right” can land as a slap. In both cases, every word has been translated correctly.

A thin wall and a thick one

Seen this way, the walls are not all the same thickness.

Chinese is separated from other East Asian languages, such as Japanese and Korean, by one thin layer. The languages are unrelated in their grammar, but they share a great deal underneath. Large parts of Korean and Japanese vocabulary were borrowed from Chinese, many of them centuries ago. More importantly, the three share many expectations about age, hierarchy, family, face and indirectness. A Chinese speaker learning Korean finds the words difficult but the social logic behind them oddly familiar.

The East Asian language sphere as a whole, though, is separated from the languages of the Latin alphabet by a thick layer. Here the differences go all the way down: in how the self is understood, in how relationships are named, in what counts as polite, honest or rude. Learning English or French, the difficulty is not only the words. It is that the assumptions behind the words are different too. You can master the grammar of a sentence and still not know whether it is the right sentence to say to this person, in this room, at this moment.

What culture shock is

This, I think, is what culture shock actually is, in its most concrete form. It is not the moment when you cannot find the right word. Translation software will find it for you. It is the moment when the words work perfectly and something still goes wrong: a request that sounds rude, a refusal you didn’t recognize as one, a friendliness that turns out to mean less than you thought. The message arrives intact. What it means on the other side is something else.

That is the sense in which language is something like a spell. A spell works only on those who share it. Inside, a few words can summon a whole world of meaning. Outside, they are only sounds, however perfectly they have been translated.
