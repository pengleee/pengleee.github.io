Optimizing the language of a hospital visit

Based on having a tooth pulled at a hospital in Shenzhen

30 July 2023

Contents

  2. Language can be optimized too
  3. Context over structure
  4. Those who know, know
  5. Postscript

I have been to this hospital in Shenzhen several times now for a tooth extraction, and only on this visit did I finally understand how a visit here actually works. So after paying, instead of going home, I sat down in the hospital for another forty-five minutes and wrote the whole process out. Below is the result, and then some thoughts on why it took me so long.

How a visit works

  1. Register. Book online, or register in person and take your queue number at the same time. Bring your medical insurance card (the electronic one on your phone works) or another proof of appointment.
    • An extraction needs two visits. The first, ordinary registration is the initial consultation: about half an hour, including a panoramic X-ray of your teeth. Because the extraction itself takes longer, you then arrange a separate time with the doctor. That second visit uses an added slot, booked outside the normal schedule, and counts as a second consultation.
  2. Take your number at the hospital and receive an appointment slip. Registration has its own fee, which insurance can cover.
  3. Go to the department printed on the slip.
  4. See the doctor. After examining or treating you, the doctor issues a guide slip listing the treatment and what it costs.
  5. Pay, and receive a receipt. The receipt is not just proof of payment: it is also what authorizes the department to carry out the treatment.
    • Shenzhen’s second-tier medical insurance only works at the hospital after a referral from the community health centre you are registered with. In that case you pay at a staffed window, not a machine, and bring both the referral slip and the guide slip.
    • For a complicated treatment you pay first and are treated afterwards. For a simple one you are treated first and pay at this step.
  6. Collect your medicine. If any of it is used during the treatment itself, collect it before the treatment.
  7. Leave.

Everything you need from all this paper comes down to four things: when, where (where you are treated, where you pay, where you collect medicine), what treatment, and how much. And the paper itself comes down to three slips, four if you need a referral: the appointment slip, the guide slip, the receipt, and the referral slip.

Language can be optimized too

Before coming to the hospital, I had lunch with a mentor, and we talked about optimization in everyday life. Most of it was the mathematical kind, the operations research that schedules deliveries and assigns resources, which I don’t really understand, and some of it was about who actually wants these things optimized and will pay for it.

Sitting in the hospital afterwards, it struck me that language gets optimized as well. Two of the most famous examples point in opposite directions. Newspeak, the official language in George Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four, is optimized to restrain thought: by shrinking the vocabulary year by year, the regime hopes to make dissent literally unsayable. Esperanto, the language L. L. Zamenhof published in 1887, is optimized for the opposite end: a regular grammar, no exceptions, built so that people with different first languages can learn it quickly and talk to each other.

Optimization always has a goal. Newspeak and Esperanto are both carefully engineered; they simply serve different goals. The list above is a very small optimization of the same kind. Its goal is to get a first-time patient from the entrance to the exit without standing in the wrong queue.

Context over structure

Why did the process need writing out at all? Partly because of how Chinese works, I think. Chinese does not put much weight on structural logic. Linguists sometimes describe the difference with a pair of terms. English is hypotactic: it ties clauses together with explicit connectives, words like because, after, unless and only if, so the logical relations sit on the surface of the sentence. Chinese is more paratactic: it can set clauses side by side and let meaning and context join them. A sign that lists “register, pay, collect medicine” in a row is perfectly good Chinese. The reader is trusted to supply the after, the because and the only if.

When I asked ChatGPT about this, its summary matched my intuition. On the whole, it said, Chinese emphasizes the overall meaning of a sentence and the coherence of information in context, rather than strict grammatical structure. That makes it flexible for abstract ideas and for feelings, but it asks the reader or listener to grasp the context carefully. Chinese grammar is relatively simple and relies on particles and context to express logical relations. English grammar is more complex, with tense, person and number, and a fixed word order.

For a newcomer, that is exactly where the difficulty lies. The context the sentences rely on is the one thing a first-time patient does not have. That the receipt doubles as a permission slip, or that the referral has to exist before you arrive, is obvious only to someone who has already done it.

Those who know, know

And yet I don’t think the hospital is doing anything wrong, and I don’t think the answer is to optimize every sentence. There is no need to spend a long time optimizing language every time. For most of life there is simply no demand for it. Chinese has a catchphrase for this: those who know, know. The staff know the process, the regular patients know the process, and for them the short version is faster and perfectly clear. Weight on context, little on structure: joining ideas by sense alone is enough.

Optimization is worth the effort only when a reader is missing the context, and only that reader knows they are missing it. That is why a patient like me ends up doing it, forty-five minutes on a hospital bench, after the fact.

Postscript

Writing this, I recognize myself as someone who badly wants to optimize life and, for now, lacks the ability to do it. I want to do many things at once and see the results immediately. Distraction and a lack of focus are another big problem.

What I need is a set of default decisions for daily life, like a preflop strategy in poker. Before any shared cards are dealt, good players don’t agonize over each hand; they follow a default chart of what to play from which position and save their attention for the decisions that are genuinely hard. A good default is a little like the list at the top of this page: worked out once, carefully, so that afterwards no one has to think about it again.
