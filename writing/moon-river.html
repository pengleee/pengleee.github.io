A translation of “Moon River”

6 December 2021

Contents
  1. How a high school student meets Audrey Hepburn
  2. Translating a song
  3. The river comes back

A few days ago, digging through the dusty heap of a WPS cloud document I had long stopped using, I found a small translation I had forgotten about: Johnny Mercer’s “Moon River”, rendered into Chinese and signed with my pen name, Peifeng. I probably finished it at the end of 2019, before anyone had heard of COVID-19. I was a high school senior then, in the busiest weeks of revising for Zhejiang’s January 2020 exams, the first of the province’s two sittings of the college entrance exams.

How a high school student meets Audrey Hepburn

I remember that our English textbook had a unit built around George Bernard Shaw’s Pygmalion, the play in which a professor of phonetics, Henry Higgins, bets that he can pass off a London flower girl, Eliza Doolittle, as a duchess simply by changing the way she speaks. The play was later turned into the musical film My Fair Lady, with Audrey Hepburn as Eliza, and our English teacher played it for us in class.

For a long time afterwards I was rather taken with Hepburn. Later I watched Breakfast at Tiffany’s with a friend, and there was “Moon River”, the song she sings sitting on a fire escape with a guitar. It moved me, and I tried translating the lyrics for that friend.

It was not, strictly speaking, a sensible use of time in those weeks. But a song that has moved you asks to be carried across, and translation is one way of saying to someone: this is what I heard. I heard that Henry Mancini wrote the melody especially for her. Her voice was lovely but did not have a wide range, so the tune was kept within it, and she could sing it on screen simply, truthfully and beautifully.

The song outlived the film. Mancini and the lyricist, Johnny Mercer, won an Academy Award for it, and it has since been recorded by so many singers that many people who know it by heart have never seen the film at all. It is one of those songs that seem to have always existed.

Translating a song

The lyrics are short, and on the surface they are simple. They are built from a handful of images: a wide river the singer means to cross one day, two drifters setting out together to see the world, a rainbow’s end they are both chasing, and, at the very end, a huckleberry friend.

Translating a poem is hard enough. Translating a song adds another constraint: whatever you write has to fit the music. Every line has a fixed number of beats, and Chinese, where each syllable is a whole character, fills those beats very differently from English. A word like “drifter”, two light syllables in English, carries a whole picture of rootless, wandering life. In Chinese you have to choose which part of that picture to keep, because there is no room for all of it.

The hardest word was the last one. My translation came with a single footnote, and it was about huckleberry. Literally, a huckleberry is a North American berry, the common name for a group of plants in the heather family, and the state fruit of Idaho. It is also the name of Mark Twain’s runaway boy, Huckleberry Finn. Because of that rustic flavour, it has come to describe someone innocent and a little mischievous, someone you have known since childhood, someone you are deeply fond of. None of that survives if you simply translate the name of the berry.

There is no Chinese berry that carries the same childhood, and no Chinese boy who floats down the same river on a raft. A translator can borrow the foreign word and lose the reader, or replace it with a local image and lose the song’s America. Either way, something is left on the far bank. So the line itself had to use a plain word for a close friend, short enough to sing, and everything else went into the footnote. I think that is what footnotes are for in translation. They are where the meaning goes when the music has used up all the syllables.

The river comes back

The river of time keeps running, and now and then it meets this moon river again.

On 28 November, at the university’s anniversary concert, the orchestra played “Moon River”. It sounded solemn and joyful at once, plain and elegant. Then, after coming to Guangdong, I discovered that China Mobile’s default ringback tone here, the music you hear while waiting for someone to pick up, is “Moon River” too. I could only marvel at the coincidence. A song I once spent hours with as a student is now something thousands of strangers hear every day while they wait.

Finding the translation brought back the nights when I listened to the song over and over, with moonlight like water. It also brought back the person I had in mind when I wrote that footnote: the friend I watched the film with and made the translation for, whom I no longer see. The song’s two drifters set out together; it never says whether they stay together.

There is nothing to be done about it. The river keeps running.
