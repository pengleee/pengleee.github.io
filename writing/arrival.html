Arrival: a language not linear in time

25 October 2024

Contents
  1. A language that changes how you think
  2. The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis
  3. Time as a loop
  4. Arrival and “The Man Who Walks Drunk”
  5. The foundation of civilization

Cause is effect, and effect is cause. That is the idea at the heart of Arrival, Denis Villeneuve’s 2016 film, adapted from Ted Chiang’s story “Story of Your Life”. It is a film about aliens, but more than that, it is a film about what learning a language might do to a mind.

A language that changes how you think

Twelve alien ships appear around the world. The linguist Louise Banks is brought in to find out what the visitors, seven-limbed creatures the humans call heptapods, want. Their written language turns out to be unlike anything on Earth. Each sentence is a single circular figure, drawn all at once, like the circle a Zen painter makes in one brushstroke. There is no first word and no last word. The writer must know the whole sentence before beginning to write it.

As Louise becomes fluent, something changes in her. She begins to experience her life the way the heptapods write their sentences: not one moment after another, but all at once. She starts to “see the future”.

The idea behind this is called linguistic relativity. The more fluent you are in a language, the more you can think in it, and so the language changes the way you think. In the film, the heptapods’ writing changes Louise’s mind, then her perception of time, and finally lets her foresee what is coming.

The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis

The physicist Ian, working alongside Louise, brings up the idea by name: the Whorf hypothesis, or in full, the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, after the linguist Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. Its basic claim is that different languages determine different ways of thinking.

It is worth being clear that this is still a hypothesis. In its strongest form, the idea that your language fixes what you are able to think, it is rejected by most linguists today: people clearly can think thoughts their language has no ready words for. Weaker versions, which say only that a language nudges its speakers to notice some things more than others, are still being studied and debated. Arrival takes the strong version and pushes it as far as it will go. That is what science fiction is for.

Time as a loop

The heptapods’ circular, nonlinear writing shows a way of thinking utterly different from ours. And so time, which in human cognition is a line running from past to future, can close into a loop.

In this loop, round like the painter’s circle, the traditional view of time is overturned. Before and after, cause and effect, no longer mean anything. Every event in the loop is at once the cause of another event and its effect. This is what allows the heptapods a perception beyond ours. They do not only perceive things in time. They perceive time itself.

The film draws a very human conclusion from all this. Louise chooses a future she already knows will bring great loss, because the whole of it, loss included, is worth having. And along the way the film offers a line that stays with me: war does not make winners, only widows.

Arrival and “The Man Who Walks Drunk”

Watching Arrival, I kept thinking of a story by the Japanese writer Kobayashi Yasumi, Suiho suru otoko, which I would translate as “The Man Who Walks Drunk”. Its premise is this: what if time does not in fact flow objectively from the past into the future, but is only an order that the human brain imposes so that it can make sense of the world? And then: what happens if a person loses the ability to impose it?

Put the two stories side by side and they arrive at the same place from different directions. Time is, in itself, neither linear nor nonlinear. Those are human understandings of it, reached either through language or through some faculty of the brain. Arrival takes the route through language: learn a different way of writing, and time rearranges itself around you. Kobayashi takes the route through the brain: lose the faculty that lines moments up, and they stop lining up. In both, the straight line of time turns out to be something we bring to the world, not something we find in it.

The foundation of civilization

The film gives Louise a thought about language that I wrote down in Chinese, and that comes back into English roughly like this: language is the cornerstone of human civilization, what binds a people together, and the vanguard of every conflict. Over the course of the film, all three turn out to be true at once. A language divides the world’s nations when they cannot share what they have learned, unites them when they finally do, and changes the mind of the one person who learns it completely.

Human civilization rests on language. Arrival simply asks how far down the foundations go.
