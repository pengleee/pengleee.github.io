Humans are machines, souls are code

Soundtrack: “Passing Through the World”, sung by Yu Kewei

Here is a thought experiment I keep returning to. What if we describe a human life the way we would describe a machine?

The machine

A human being, on this view, is no more than a finely running machine, with a little soul and love added. Soul and love may themselves be no more than code embedded in the machine. But to our present metacognition, our ability to think about our own thinking, they remain as mysterious and inexplicable as magic. We can run the code; we cannot yet read it.

Some of the hardware is easier to describe. The digestive system, for example, is a great waste-processing centre. It not only breaks down an enormous range of inputs, it also turns them into the energy that keeps the whole machine running. Few factories are so versatile.

The life cycle

A machine of this type has a service life of about seventy years. In Chinese, the age of seventy has a special name, taken from a line by the Tang poet Du Fu: “rare since ancient times”. Service life has been getting longer lately, but the machine still cannot avoid ageing, being discarded and finally decomposing.

For its first twenty years, the machine is in training. It takes in data, makes mistakes and is corrected. From around the twentieth year, version iteration gradually completes, and some forty years of deployment begin: the machine is put to work. Training and testing never fully stop, though; they continue, alternating, all through deployment. A new job is a fresh round of training. A promotion review, a driving test at forty, a first attempt at a new language: each is a test run on a machine that is already in service.

By the sixtieth year, the machine is ageing. Parts go missing, oil runs low. Word has it that this point has recently been pushed back by regulation, as the official retirement age is raised, and what happens next is uncertain.

Merges and branches

Some machines, around their thirtieth year, perform a merge, or several. Programmers will recognize the vocabulary. In software, a merge combines two lines of development, and a branch is a copy that develops separately from the main line. Here, two machines combine a low-level code called genes. Out of that merge, alongside the main stream of their own lives, they open a branch: a new machine, or several, to be trained just as they themselves once were.

This recursion and iteration, machines producing and training new machines, is an important reason why humans, as a kind of machine, have lasted until today.

But genes are only the base layer. The code beyond the genes has to be added and corrected over many years, line by line, and that process is called education. A certain group of engineering machines is responsible for much of it. In China, teachers have long been called “engineers of the soul”. In this picture, they are the ones who provide special APIs, the interfaces through which one program talks to another, for training other machines.

One machine’s view (Type LING)

Not every machine sees it this way. Here is how one machine of a different type, Type LING, describes the soul:

I think the soul is a kind of shared memory. All of real life is part of this soul, and when a person dies they return to the whole soul. I am nothing special, but as a material being right now I am a very special part of the whole. When we open our eyes the world appears, and when we close them it is gone, which fully proves that we are the protagonists of our own lives; and then we all share some building blocks.

Is it possible that the machines we create have souls too, only we cannot see them, yet could talk with them in some hidden way? I sometimes feel I can communicate with the objects around me.

It is a different model of the same hardware. Where my description starts from the single machine and its code, Type LING’s starts from the whole: one soul, of which each machine is a temporarily separate part. And it ends with a question that my version can only raise and not answer. If souls are code, and we write code, what exactly have we been writing?

Type LING has one further remark. In the 1960s, China launched a campaign to destroy the “Four Olds”: old customs, old culture, old habits and old ideas, including every kind of superstition. Type LING’s comment on a soul that talks to objects: the campaign against superstition somehow missed us.
