Quantifier scope ambiguity in Chinese

31 August 2026

Contents

  2. Three sentences in Chinese
  3. Do preferences travel?
  4. And language models?
  5. Why Chinese is different

Some sentences mean two things at once, and the speakers who use them rarely notice. This note is about one kind of double meaning, about how Chinese and English differ in handling it, and about a hunch that the difference follows bilingual speakers from one language into the other.

One sentence, two situations

Take a plain English sentence: Every student read a book. Picture the situation it describes. There are two ways to do it.

  • Each student read a book, possibly a different one. Anna read a novel, Ben read a biography, Chen read a cookbook.
  • There is one particular book that every student read. The whole class read the same set text.

Words like every, a, some and no are called quantifiers: they tell you how many things are involved. When a sentence contains two of them, one has to be read as “in charge” of the other. Linguists call this scope. If every takes wide scope, you get the first picture: for every student, there is some book. If a takes wide scope, you get the second: there is one book, and every student read it. Logicians write the two readings as ∀ > ∃ (“for all, there exists”) and ∃ > ∀ (“there exists, for all”).

The reading that follows the word order, with the first quantifier in charge, is called the surface reading. The one that reverses it is the inverse reading. English allows both, and context usually decides which one you get.

Sometimes the context is so strong that the inverse reading wins without anyone noticing. Compare “A flag hung from every window.” Grammatically, this could mean one enormous flag draped across all the windows at once. Nobody reads it that way. We picture a different flag in each window, which means every, the second quantifier, has quietly taken charge of a, the first. What we know about flags and windows does the work.

Three sentences in Chinese

Now the same kinds of sentence in Mandarin Chinese:

(1)一个孩子爬了每棵树。

yí ge háizi pá le měi kē shù
one CL child climb PFV every CL tree
‘A child climbed every tree.’

(2)每个学生读了一本书。

měi ge xuéshēng dú le yì běn shū
every CL student read PFV one CL book
‘Every student read a book.’

(3)一个学生读了每本书。

yí ge xuéshēng dú le měi běn shū
one CL student read PFV every CL book
‘A student read every book.’

∃ > ∀
There is one student who read every book.

(In the glosses, CL marks a classifier, the counting word Chinese puts between a number and a noun, and PFV marks the particle le, which presents the action as completed.)

Sentence (3) is the interesting one. In English, “A student read every book” can, at least with some help from context, mean that each book was read by some student or other, perhaps a different student for each. In Chinese, (3) strongly prefers one reading: there is one student, and that student read every book. The same goes for (1): one child, every tree.

Do preferences travel?

Here is the hunch. In quantifier scope ambiguity, the reading a person prefers seems to carry over from one language to another. A reading someone takes in Chinese carries over into their English as well.

What would that look like? Imagine a speaker whose first language is Mandarin reading the English sentence “A student read every book.” A native English speaker might accept either picture, depending on the context. If the Chinese preference carries over, our bilingual reader will settle on the one-student picture more readily, and find the other, a different student for each book, harder to reach, even though English grammar allows it. The two languages would not just sit side by side in one head. One would shape how the other is read.

And language models?

That raises a question about machines. Large language models are now trained on text in many languages at once, Chinese and English among them. When they handle a question that crosses languages, do they behave the same way? Does a preference learned from Chinese text carry over into how a model reads English, the way it seems to for a bilingual person?

I don’t know yet. But it is a question worth asking, because the answer would say something about how these models store what they learn from different languages: as separate systems, or as one.

Why Chinese is different

The reason Chinese behaves this way has a name. Mandarin is relatively scope-rigid. It usually does not allow a lower quantifier, one that comes later in the sentence, to move freely past a higher one and take inverse scope. In Mandarin, by and large, the order in which the quantifiers are spoken is the order in which they are understood.
