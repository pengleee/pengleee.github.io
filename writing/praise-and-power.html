Praise is also a discipline of power

19 October 2022

Contents
  1. 19 October 2022
  2. 28 October 2022

19 October 2022

A cleaner at a certain university library has become briefly famous. During a break from work he sat down to read a book. A student took a photo, the photo travelled, and it happened to reach the university’s president, who praised him warmly in a post on WeChat Moments, the Chinese equivalent of a Facebook timeline.

The praise is fine, of course. Reading is a noble thing. But I always seem to need to show a little childish contrariness over some trivial matter, and this is one of them.

Criticism and praise

The Great Gatsby opens with the narrator’s father telling him: “Whenever you feel like criticizing any one, just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.” The word in the original is criticize, which, taken out of context, could also be translated more neutrally as “comment on”. And once you read it that way, the advice covers far more than criticism. It covers praise too.

Whether it is criticism and rebuke or praise and admiration, what stands behind it is what I can only call a discipline of power. The word discipline here is Michel Foucault’s: the quiet ways in which people are shaped, not by force, but by being observed, ranked, corrected and held up as examples. The person who comments usually holds the advantage of status. And the comment is usually made on the basis of some fixed stereotype of the person being commented on.

Praise is the harder case to see, because it feels kind. But consider what this particular praise needs in order to make sense. A cleaner reading in a library is newsworthy only if we assume that cleaners don’t normally read. The compliment “how admirable, a cleaner who reads” carries a hidden second half: for a cleaner.

What I assumed

I am not outside this. I have often seen the same cleaner reading in the library these past few days. The first time, I took a photo too. My first thought was: how novel, a cleaner actually reading. My second thought was: I don’t seem to have read many books in this library myself, and I fall short of him.

After seeing him again and again, a trace of guilt and self-examination crept in. Not having read enough was the lesser part. Mainly I found myself asking: why did I assume that a cleaner would not read in the library, and treat his reading as a novelty? Probably because of a long-standing stereotype of cleaners as people who toil from dawn to dusk with no time for culture, together with the selfish sense of superiority that comes with being a student at this university. Both my photo and the president’s praise grew from the same soil.

28 October 2022

Since the president’s post I have kept coming to the library every day, and I can no longer find the cleaner who loved to read. Does he know that for a moment he was praised by a distinguished academician and held up as a model for the whole student body? Is this the cleaners’ version of the “Nobel curse”, where the honour itself seems to bring the work to a stop? I am probably overthinking it. But the fact is that I have not seen him these past few days.

Soil and sun

There is a familiar line of encouragement in Chinese: “Even if you are soil, you will hold up tomorrow’s sun!” It is meant as encouragement, and which of the two images is lowly and which is exalted goes without saying. But why must the soil hold up the sun? Can’t the soil simply be fragrant, as soil, on its own terms?

Chinese is full of images like this. The pheasant becomes a phoenix. The carp leaps the dragon gate, a waterfall that, in legend, turns any carp that clears it into a dragon. These are earnest hopes and fine compliments, and they are offered sincerely. But they share a structure. They praise the lower thing only for its chance of becoming the higher one. They work by diminishing the subject’s own worth as what it is, and their final aim is assimilation to something else. In that sense they too are a discipline of power, exercised from a god’s-eye view over those of lower status.

This discipline need not be intentional. It can be pervasive and metaphorical, built into the language of encouragement itself. And the binary logic does damage at both ends. It leads the soil to pin its only glory on the sun. It leads the phoenix to mock the pheasant it used to be, and the soaring dragon to look down on the carp. The god on high, meanwhile, simply stays on high.

A transitive friend

As for me, I have stolen some time lately to put on a show of reading. First I borrowed Bashō’s The Narrow Road to the Deep North and carried it with me every day until I had read six or seven tenths of it. Then I returned it. If I want to read a book, why own it? So it went back on the shelf for others to visit.

On the Japanese literature shelf I found an old friend from high school, a collection of Dazai Osamu’s stories. I have just finished “The Flowers of Buffoonery” and am now reading Tsugaru, his account of a journey back to the region where he grew up. It is warm and bright, full of Dazai’s love for his home and of the small teasing that such deep love brings, quite unlike most of his writing.

Every day, the first thing I do in the library is take the book down from its fixed place and read a section before starting work. The last thing before I leave is to put it back in that loyal, unchanging spot. It is as if I have gained a friend to work and rest with each day. And if one day this friend goes elsewhere and meets new readers, I will be glad too. Then I will have a transitive friend, a friendship that passes along from reader to reader, and no need to possess it.

I have another field of study. People provisionally call it “love”. It is a field that studies how human hearts communicate with one another.

Dazai Osamu, Tsugaru
