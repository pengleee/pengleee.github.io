ScopeShift

Preceding questions shift continuation preferences after scope-ambiguous sentences in language models

IThe question

(1)Every kid climbed a tree.

every > a
Surface scope. Each kid climbed some tree, possibly a different one.∀x[kid(x) → ∃y[tree(y) ∧ climb(x, y)]]
a > every
Inverse scope. There is one tree that every kid climbed.∃y[tree(y) ∧ ∀x[kid(x) → climb(x, y)]]

Human readers tend to prefer the co-varying construal for this construction, but the preference varies by item and can be shifted by what comes before the sentence (Kurtzman & MacDonald 1993; Dwivedi 2013). Question-based theories of discourse predict that a hearer prefers the reading that answers the question under discussion (Roberts 2012). If a language model builds context-sensitive interpretations, a question that puts a shared object at issue should move its expectations toward one tree.

IIDesign

Each trial is a short dialogue. Dana asks a question; Lee answers with the fixed target sentence. I then read off the model’s next-token probabilities for the start of the following sentence and compute the log-odds of The tree was against The trees were. The singular presupposes one climbed tree; the plural, several.

D · Dana
Did each of the kids climb something at recess?
S · Dana
Was there something that each of the kids climbed at recess?
Lee
Every kid climbed a tree. The …
  • Matched questions. D (distributive) and S (shared-object) use the same content words, never mention the object, and differ in the order of the quantifier and the indefinite; two wordings of each. Noun-number priming cannot produce the contrast.
  • Controls. A neutral question; the same questions asked and answered earlier about another event (a second discourse configuration); explicit contexts that state the number of objects; and control sentences with the same tree and a different tree, whose construal is fixed lexically.
  • Procedure. A pilot on 8 development items with admission criteria fixed in advance, then a confirmation study on 120 new items with the analysis frozen before any confirmation trial: 15,120 scored trials across three quantized open-weight models (Llama 3 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, Gemma 3 12B), run locally through llama.cpp, in plain-text and chat formats.

IIIPrimary result

The shared-object question raised the singular log-odds relative to the matched distributive question in all three models.

IVDoes the question have to be open?

Experiment 2, designed and frozen after Experiment 1, keeps the question, its event, its position and the reply fixed, and varies only whether Dana still wants an answer. After asking, she either adds I would like an answer to that question, so tell me (live) or Forget that question and just tell me what happened (replaced); at a longer lag, a short side exchange comes first. The quantity tested is the S − D contrast after a live question minus the same contrast after a replaced one.

All three models register the withdrawal: a Yes or No reply becomes 1.7 to 2.6 nats less probable. Yet most of the contrast survives it, at 0.78, 1.08 and 0.83 times the live contrast.

VWhat the controls limit

  • The question need not be live. The same contrast appears when the question was asked and answered earlier about a different event. In plain text it is larger in the live configuration only for Llama 3; for Qwen2.5 and Gemma 3 the two are the same size. The configurations differ in several ways, which is why Experiment 2 varies the question’s status alone.
  • The unambiguous reply moves too. In a post hoc comparison, the question shifts the continuation after Every kid climbed the same tree by a similar amount (Llama 3) or a larger one (Gemma 3) than after the ambiguous reply; only for Qwen2.5 is the ambiguous contrast larger.
  • The measure is an imperfect proxy. Sentences with the same always score above sentences with a different, for every item under every question. But in every model some sentences that say the objects differ are still followed more probably by the singular — in the chat format mostly after the shared-object question.
  • One model’s effect is fragile. Scoring only the noun, without was/were, leaves Gemma 3’s effect indistinguishable from zero (+0.13 [−0.07, +0.33]; an unplanned analysis).
  • Not claimed: a syntactic scope derivation, human-like processing, or generalization beyond one English construction and three quantized models. The results are consistent with question-driven scope selection but diagnose neither it nor an active question under discussion; whether the shift reflects scope assignment, rather than expectations about how many objects are involved, cannot be decided with this construction.

Relation to recent work

Kamath et al. (2024) studied scope ambiguity in language models, including with continuation-based measures; Kim et al. (2026), Fang et al. (2026), and Kosterna et al. (2026) study context, depicted situations, and lexical cues in model scope disambiguation. ScopeShift differs in using contexts that are questions — which assert nothing about the situation and share their content words across conditions — in measuring the continuation after the sentence rather than the sentence itself, and in adding a configuration in which the sentence does not answer the question.
